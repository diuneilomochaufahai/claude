# Lockheed Constellation — Presentation Script

Plain-English speaker script, one section per slide. Lines in parentheses are presenter notes, not spoken.

## 1. Title

Hi everyone. Quick question to start: if a long trip was actually something you looked forward to, what kind of plane would you want to be on?

Today I want to show you the Lockheed Constellation. People called it "Connie". I'll go through three parts: who built it and what it could do, what it was used for, and why it still matters.

(Pause for two seconds, then move on.)

## 2. Part 1 divider

Part one. Who built it, where, and what the plane could actually do.

## 3. Name and location

The plane was built by Lockheed, at their plant in Burbank, California, just outside Los Angeles.

"Constellation" is a whole family, not one plane. It starts with the L-049 in the 1940s and ends with the L-1649A Starliner in the late 1950s. When I show numbers today, I'll tell you which model they come from.

(Note for the presenter: Burbank is Lockheed's historic home plant; the source pages list it under general Lockheed history. Reference: lm.)

## 4. Meet Connie

Here are the four numbers to remember. About 13,600 horsepower from four engines. A cruising speed of roughly 480 to 500 kilometers an hour. A range of about 9,500 to 10,000 kilometers. And a wingspan of 123 feet.

Don't worry about memorizing them. Each one comes back in the next few slides.

(Power, speed and range: L-1649A Super Star, Lufthansa data. Wingspan: HB-RSC Super Constellation, SCFA data. References: lh, scfa.)

## 5. Size and weight

How big was it? The fuselage is about 116 feet long, that's around 35 meters. The wings reach 123 feet tip to tip, about 37 meters. And at takeoff it could weigh around 70 tonnes.

The easy way to picture it: the plane is longer than a basketball court, and the wings are wider than one.

(Presenter note: wingspan is the HB-RSC Super Constellation figure from SCFA. Length and takeoff weight are approximate Super Constellation / Starliner figures from general references, so double-check them before the talk if you want exact numbers. The three tail fins were there so the plane could fit in existing hangars.)

## 6. Wingspan vs the first flight

Here's a fun way to feel that size. In 1903, the Wright brothers' first flight covered 120 feet. The whole flight.

This plane's wingspan, tip to tip, is 123 feet. The two lines are drawn to the same scale, and they're only three feet apart.

So the entire distance of the first flight in history is shorter than the width of this one plane. That's how far aviation came in about fifty years.

(Say "full wingspan", tip to tip. Don't exaggerate the gap, it's only 2.5 percent. References: scfa, nps.)

## 7. Engines

So how did it fly so far? Four engines. Each circle is one engine, a Wright R-3350. On the later Lufthansa Super Star, each one gave about 3,400 horsepower. Four of them together, about 13,600.

Each engine has 18 cylinders in two rows. That's a lot of power for a propeller plane, and it pushed these planes about as far as propellers could go. The downside: that many cylinders means a lot of maintenance.

(Point to the four engines one by one, then the total. This figure is for the L-1649A, not every early Connie. References: lh, lm.)

## 8. Performance: one minute

480 to 500 kilometers an hour sounds a bit abstract. Try this instead. In the time it takes me to talk for one minute, the plane has moved about eight kilometers.

That's cruising speed with no wind. Top speed and cruising speed are different things, so I'm not using the fastest number as if the plane held it the whole way.

(Each block is one kilometer. Only stress "one minute, about eight kilometers". Reference: lh.)

## 9. Fuel economy

Let's talk about fuel, because this is where piston airliners hurt. The Starliner carried roughly 36,000 liters of fuel, about 9,600 US gallons, to fly its full range of around 10,000 kilometers.

Divide one by the other and you get about three and a half liters per kilometer. For a plane carrying around 60 to 90 people, that was good for its time. But it's a reminder of why airlines jumped at jets a few years later: jets could carry more people, faster, for less fuel per seat.

(Presenter note: the fuel capacity is an approximate Starliner figure from general references, not from the listed source pages. Verify before quoting it exactly. The "bathtub" line is just a picture: a bathtub holds roughly 200 liters.)

## 10. New, used and after-market

You can't buy a new Constellation. The last one was built in 1958. So the only market today is used planes and restoration, and this slide shows what that costs.

In 2007, a Lufthansa-related group bought an old Super Star airframe in the United States. The dream was to get it flying again. In 2018 they stopped that plan. In 2019 the plane was shipped back to Germany. Reports quoting Lufthansa's CEO put the total cost at about 150 million euros, including donations and sponsorship. In 2025 Lufthansa finished it as a non-flying display.

So the price of keeping a legend alive was 150 million euros, and even then, it doesn't fly.

(The 150 million covers 2007 to 2019 only. It is not the plane's market value. References: cost, restore.)

## 11. Challenges: 1939

Now the best part of the story. The challenges this plane faced weren't only technical. They were about business, and about war.

It's 1939. Every airline is flying the same old planes, like the DC-3. Slow, and all the same. Everybody is waiting politely in the same line.

(Slow down here. This is the start of the climax. Let "1939" sit for a second.)

## 12. Challenges: the secret contract

Then Howard Hughes, who controlled the airline TWA, makes his move. He goes to Lockheed quietly and gets them to design a new plane in secret. And he adds one key condition.

Lockheed could not sell the plane to any other big US airline until TWA had received 35 of them. That's the gate you see on the slide. Thirty-five blocks, one for each plane.

Think of it like a friend who works at the club telling you which table is about to open up, before anyone else knows. Hughes got the inside track, and locked it in with a contract.

(The 35 blocks show the agreement, not a claim that 35 planes were delivered before the war. The rule only applied to other US transcontinental airlines. Reference: lm.)

## 13. Challenges: Pan Am waits

Meanwhile, Pan Am's boss, Juan Trippe, is doing things the normal way. Slowly talking to Boeing, talking to Douglas, comparing offers.

He has no idea the race is already over. When Lockheed finally announces the Constellation, Trippe realizes he's been beaten. Even if he orders right now, he has to wait years, because TWA is already at the front of the line with 35 planes.

Picture him standing there with a glass of champagne, watching the other guy walk out the door with the prize.

(Point to TWA at the front, then sweep along the line. This is the laugh line, keep it light. The Trippe framing is storytelling; the documented fact is the 35-plane clause. Reference: lm.)

## 14. Challenges: the black swan

But then, the thing nobody planned for. December 1941. Pearl Harbor. The United States goes to war, and the military takes over the Constellation production line. Every plane becomes a military transport, the C-69.

Hughes's head start disappears in a second. He'd locked in the prize, and the government walked in and said: we'll take all of it. His exclusive contract was now just a piece of paper. After the war, every airline lined up again from the start.

So Hughes thought he'd played it like a Wall Street genius, and history slapped him awake. Lesson learned.

(Pause after "Pearl Harbor." Drop your voice for the last line. References: lm, nas.)

## 15. Challenges: the jets arrive

The last big challenge was time itself. By the late 1950s a new era had arrived. The Super Star cruised at about 480 to 500 kilometers an hour. The Boeing 707 jet cruised at 880 to 900. Same scale on both bars. Almost double.

Lufthansa got its Super Stars in 1957. By 1960, only three years later, Lufthansa was already flying 707s. The best propeller airliner ever built had a very short window at the top.

(The thick bars show cruise ranges. Don't mix the lowest and highest numbers to make the gap look bigger. This compares speed, not flight time. Reference: lh.)

## 16. Design and testing

Let's look at the design. Flying far is one thing. Flying far and feeling fine is another. Two systems made that happen.

Top half, for passengers: a pressurized cabin. The air inside is kept at a higher pressure than the thin air outside, so you can fly high, above a lot of the bad weather, and still breathe normally.

Bottom half, for pilots: hydraulic boost. The pilot moves the controls, the hydraulics multiply that force, and the big control surfaces move. That's how two people could fly a 70-tonne plane with their hands.

The design was developed in secret for Hughes, and the prototype was tested by the Army Air Forces during the war. Pressurization doesn't mean zero turbulence, and the Constellation wasn't the first pressurized airliner, the Boeing 307 was. What Connie did was put it all together into one long-distance package.

(Top is passengers, bottom is pilots. The pressure graphic is a concept, not real numbers. References: lm, nas, sfo.)

## 17. First flight

The first flight was on January 9, 1943. Because of the war, the prototype didn't fly in airline colors. It flew as a military transport, the C-69, from Lockheed's field in Burbank.

So the plane that was designed in secret for an airline made its first flight wearing a uniform.

(Reference: lm, nas.)

## 18. Key milestones: timeline

Here's the whole story on one line. 1939, Hughes starts the secret project. 1941, the US enters the war. 1942, the military takes over production. 1943, first flight as the C-69. And only in February 1946 does TWA finally start flying passengers across the Atlantic with it.

The middle part of this line is why the plane's launch was so much messier than anyone planned.

(Go left to right. The line is to scale by year. References: lm, twa, nas.)

## 19. Key milestones: the family

Three more milestones, because Connie kept changing. The early L-049 went into airline service right after the war. In 1951 came the L-1049 Super Constellation, with a longer body for more passengers. And in 1957 the L-1649A Starliner got a brand new wing for very long flights. Lufthansa called it the Super Star.

That's why I keep saying which model a number comes from. They're not all the same plane.

(Three representative models, not the full family tree. Photo: Lockheed history page. References: nas, lh, lm.)

## 20. Number produced and operators

How many were built? 856 in total, civil and military. Each block here is ten planes: eight full rows of a hundred, plus 56.

Only 44 of them, the orange ones, were the final Starliner model. About five percent. A very successful family, but the last generation got a very short run.

Who flew them? TWA was the launch customer and the airline most associated with Connie. Air France and Lufthansa flew them across the Atlantic. The US military flew hundreds as transports and radar planes.

(The last half-block is exactly 6 planes. 44 divided by 856 is about 5.1 percent. Operator list is from the photos and sources in this deck, not a ranked table; say "among the main operators" rather than "largest". References: hist, twa, lh.)

## 21. Part 2 divider

Part two. What was this plane actually used for? Three jobs, and one of them might surprise you.

## 22. Main use: the long-haul airliner

Job number one: carrying passengers a very long way, in comfort. Take a Lufthansa Super Star from Hamburg to New York. That flight could take up to 17 hours.

Seventeen hours. So some airlines fitted a lounge and real beds, sleeper berths like the ones in this photo. You'd have dinner, chat for a while, then go to sleep, and wake up in another city. The long time in the air became part of the experience instead of something to suffer through.

(Pause before pointing out dine, talk, rest. Services varied by airline. The photo is an early Constellation cabin from Lockheed's history page, not the Lufthansa plane. References: lm, lh, cabin.)

## 23. Unusual uses

Jobs two and three are the unusual ones.

In 1944, Orville Wright, one of the two brothers who made that 120-foot first flight, took his last ever flight. It was on a Constellation.

In 1953, President Eisenhower's plane, a Constellation called Columbine II, was the first aircraft to use the call sign "Air Force One".

And during the Cold War, the military fitted a big radar on top and called it the EC-121. It flew for hours as an early-warning station in the sky, watching for enemy aircraft.

(These are three different versions, not three jobs on one plane. "Air Force One" is a call sign, not a plane type. References: lm, af, ec.)

## 24. Part 3 divider

Part three. Why does this plane matter, and what did it change for the whole industry?

## 25. Significance: time

The biggest thing Connie changed was time. These two bars are TWA flights across the United States, in two different eras.

In 1930, it took 36 hours, and you slept overnight in Kansas City on the way. In 1953, a Super Constellation flew Los Angeles to New York nonstop in eight hours.

A day and a half became one working day. That changes how people think about distance. Suddenly the other coast is somewhere you can go for a meeting.

(Point to the long bar, then the short one. Both start at zero. Not all of that saving is the plane's speed; nonstop routes and better operations helped too. Reference: twa.)

## 26. Significance: the business lesson

The Constellation also changed how airlines think about buying planes. Three lessons from the Hughes story.

First: being first matters. Lock in the supply, launch before your rivals, and you set the standard for everyone.

Second: a head start is fragile. One world event, Pearl Harbor, wiped out the whole plan.

Third: the top of the market is short. The best propeller airliner was overtaken by jets in just a few years. Airlines learned to watch the next technology, not just the current one.

## 27. Closing

So back to my first question: what kind of plane would you choose?

Connie shows three things. Technology can change how long a journey takes. Good design can make the journey itself something to look forward to. And a plane's story can last a lot longer than its time on the market.

If we could go back to that era, I'd invite you to walk up the stairs and get on board. Thank you.

(Only say "thank you" on this slide.)

## 28. Q&A backup

Backup slide for questions. Four things people mix up: the Boeing 307 was pressurized first; the 35 planes were a contract condition, not actual deliveries before the war; 123 feet is the full wingspan, not one wing; and 150 million euros is a project cost, not what the plane is worth. Also: "first-mover advantage" is my way of telling the story, not a legal claim about insider dealing. (References: sfo, lm, scfa, cost.)
